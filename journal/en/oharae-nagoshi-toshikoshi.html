Oharae (June & December Great Purification): Meaning of Chinowa Rings, Katashiro, and Renewal

Published 2026-10-31 · Updated 2026-10-31 · Kamidana App Editorial Team

Twice a year, at the midpoint and close of the calendar, Shinto shrines across Japan conduct one of the tradition’s oldest ceremonies: Oharae (大祓), the Great Purification.

The ceremony on June 30 is known as Nagoshi no Harae (Midyear Purification), washing away the accumulated wear of the first half of the year, while the ceremony on December 31 is Toshikoshi no Harae (Year-End Purification), clearing spiritual stagnation before the new year arrives.

What does Shinto mean by "impurities" (kegare)? How does one walk through a giant circular grass ring (chinowa)? And how can we adopt this biannual reset into modern daily life to refresh our mental clarity? Here is everything you need to know.

1. What Is Oharae? Shinto’s Biannual Spiritual Reset

Established over a millennium ago as an imperial ritual, Oharae was performed to cleanse the entire community of unintentional mistakes, missteps, and stagnant energies gathered over six months of living.

How Shinto Understands "Tsumi" and "Kegare"

In Shinto, words translated as "transgression" (tsumi) or "impurity" (kegare) do not refer solely to moral guilt or legal offenses. They encompass a wider range of conditions and misfortunes that call for purification before approaching the kami, and scholars and traditions explain them from several perspectives:

  • Kegare (穢れ): Refers broadly to states of ritual impurity that call for cleansing, and in one well-known interpretation is connected to ki-gare (気枯れ)—a state in which vital spirit or everyday freshness has become depleted and needs renewal.
  • Tsumi (罪): Encompasses not only actions that disrupt communal harmony, but also unintentional missteps and misfortunes accumulated in the course of living.

Oharae is not a punitive rite. It is an act of ceremonial renewal—clearing away the accumulated weight of six months so that people and communities can return to an upright, refreshed state of clarity. This connects deeply with the Shinto ideal of tokowaka (常若, eternal renewal and youthfulness), the concept that spiritual vitality is sustained not through static preservation, but through rhythmic, seasonal renewal.

2. Chinowa Rings and Katashiro: Meaning and Step-by-Step Practice

Visiting a shrine during the Oharae season reveals two distinctive elements: large woven grass rings standing in the courtyard and paper figures shaped in human silhouettes.

Walking Through the Chinowa Ring (Figure-Eight Pattern)

During the summer Oharae season, many shrines erect a large ring woven from chigaya (cogon grass) in their precincts. Passing through this chinowa ring is a traditional rite for dispelling the accumulated transgressions and impurities of the half-year and praying for health and renewal.

Standard Chinowa Passing Steps:
The most widely observed traditional method across Japanese shrines follows a figure-eight pattern completed in three passes:

  1. Stand facing the chinowa ring, bow once, pass through the ring, turn left, and return to the front.
  2. Bow once, pass through the ring, turn right, and return to the front.
  3. Bow once, pass through the ring, turn left once more, and return to the front.
  4. Finally, bow once, pass straight through the ring, and proceed directly to the shrine sanctuary to offer your prayer.

At some shrines, participants quietly recite traditional verses or prayers associated with the ancient legend of Somin Shorai as they walk. Because specific routes, counts, or recitations can vary by sanctuary, always check and follow the guidance board posted beside the ring.

Katashiro: Entrusting Burdens to Paper

Katashiro (形代), or hitogata, are paper silhouettes used to receive transgressions and impurities so they can be purified.

Standard Katashiro Procedure:

  1. Write your name and age (often traditional counting age) on the paper silhouette.
  2. Gently stroke your body—such as your forehead, chest, and arms—with the paper to transfer accumulated spiritual fatigue and impurities.
  3. Breathe onto the katashiro three times, entrusting your personal burdens to the paper.
  4. Submit the katashiro to the shrine with an offering (hatsuho-ryo), where priests recite the Oharae-no-Kotoba liturgy and purify the papers through ritual burning or release to sacred waters.

3. Participating in Oharae at a Shrine

Around June 30 and December 31, many shrines set up a chinowa ring or provide katashiro papers for visitors.

Following Each Shrine's Posted Guidance

Because the specific way to walk through the chinowa ring and submit katashiro can vary by shrine and region, check the guidance board placed beside the ring or at the shrine office, walk through calmly, and proceed to the sanctuary to offer your prayer (two bows, two claps, one final bow).

5. Summary: Biannual Renewal for Mind and Heart

The enduring beauty of Oharae lies in its deep kindness toward the human condition: recognizing that fatigue and mistakes naturally happen, and providing an intentional, recurring moment to rinse them away.

  • Oharae occurs biannually on June 30 (Nagoshi) and December 31 (Toshikoshi).
  • Purification clears accumulated tsumi and kegare (often interpreted in one view as depleted vitality, ki-gare) to restore natural clarity.
  • Walking through the woven grass chinowa ring and entrusting impurities to katashiro are traditional rites of purification.
  • Through paper katashiro or the Kamidana App’s Oharai feature, releasing heavy words helps you begin the next chapter refreshed.
