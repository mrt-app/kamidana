Ofuda Guide: What It Is, Where to Place It & How to Enshrine It at Home

Published 2026-02-19 · Updated 2026-10-31 · Kamidana App Editorial Team

A Japanese friend handed you a paper talisman and said, "This is an ofuda — it's sacred, please take care of it." Or maybe a relative sent one from Japan. Now it's sitting on your shelf and you're wondering: "What exactly am I supposed to do with this?"

You're not alone. According to kamidana (home shrine) shop owners, most customers come in not because they visited a shrine themselves, but because someone gave them an ofuda and they want to enshrine it properly.

This guide covers everything you need to know: what ofuda actually are, where and how to enshrine them, what to do without a kamidana, and how to properly return them when the time comes.

Quick Guide to Ofuda at Home

  • What it is: A sacred paper or wooden Shinto talisman containing a deity's spirit (wakemitama).
  • Placement rules: Eye level or higher, facing south or east, in a clean and peaceful living space.
  • Apartments & no kamidana: A clean shelf lined with white washi paper or a simple wall stand is completely acceptable.
  • Annual renewal: Kept with reverence for one year and returned to a Shinto shrine.

What Is an Ofuda? The Three Sacred Talismans of the Kamidana

An ofuda is a sacred talisman containing a divine portion of a deity's spirit (wakemitama). Unlike omamori (protective charms you carry with you), ofuda are enshrined within a clean space in the home to welcome divine protection into daily life.

In traditional Japanese households, three distinct ofuda are commonly enshrined together. Each connects daily life to the kami in a unique and meaningful dimension:

The Three Ofuda Enshrined in the Home

  • Jingū Taima (Ise Grand Shrine / Amaterasu Ōmikami): Enshrined seeking the blessings of Amaterasu Ōmikami, the ancestral kami whose solar light nurtures all living things. As the common guardian of the nation and universal life, this talisman is distributed at shrines nationwide and placed at the very center (or foremost position) of the altar as an anchor of universal thanksgiving.
  • Ujigami Ofuda (Local Guardian Deity): Received from the guardian shrine of the land and neighborhood where you currently reside. As the deity directly safeguarding your immediate living environment, daily commutes, and local community, this talisman represents daily gratitude for peaceful neighborhood life.
  • Sūkei-jinja Ofuda (Personal Devotion Shrine): Regardless of geography or distance, shrines for which you hold deep personal devotion are called sūkei-jinja. These may include the shrine of your birthplace (ubusuna-jinja, which enshrines your natal guardian deity, ubusunagami), a sanctuary cherished by your family across generations, or a shrine you visited during a meaningful journey where you felt profound personal resonance.

Enshrining these three ofuda reflects our relationships with universal life (Jingū Taima), our physical community (Ujigami), and our personal roots and aspirations (Sūkei-jinja). Pausing before the kamidana each day is a grounding practice that gently reminds us of these sustaining connections.

Placement Rules: Direction and Height

When choosing where to place your ofuda, keep these principles in mind:

  • Above eye level: Ofuda should never be looked down upon. Place them on a high shelf, atop furniture, or mounted on a wall.
  • Facing south or east: The front of the ofuda should face south (ideal) or east. These directions are associated with sunlight and positive energy.
  • In a clean, bright space: Avoid placing ofuda near bathrooms or in cluttered areas. A living room or a bright, well-visited room is ideal.

That said, don't let the rules stop you from enshrining at all. A clean, respectful spot where you feel at peace is always better than a "perfect" direction you can't achieve.

No Kamidana? No Problem

You don't need a traditional kamidana (shrine shelf) to enshrine ofuda properly. Modern alternatives include:

  • Wall-mounted mini shelves: Designed specifically for ofuda. Many attach with pins or adhesive strips — perfect for rentals.
  • A clean shelf with white cloth: Lay a white cloth or washi paper on a bookshelf and lean the ofuda against the wall.
  • Ofuda stands: Simple wooden stands made specifically for ofuda. Compact and elegant.

What matters is reverence, not furniture. If you live in a rental or apartment, explore our guide to modern kamidana ideas for small spaces. If you treat the ofuda with care, you're already doing it right.

Stacking Order (When You Have Multiple Ofuda)

If your kamidana has three doors (sansha-zukuri):

  • Center: Jingū Taima (Ise Grand Shrine)
  • Your right: Ujigami (local shrine)
  • Your left: Sūkei-jinja (personally revered shrine)

If your kamidana has one door (issha-zukuri), stack them front to back:

  • Front: Jingū Taima
  • Middle: Ujigami
  • Back: Sūkei-jinja

Even without a kamidana, place the Jingū Taima in the most prominent position.

Returning Old Ofuda

Ofuda are typically returned and renewed after one year. The most common timing is during New Year: you receive a new ofuda and return the old one.

  • Return to the original shrine: The most respectful option. Look for the kosatsu-osamejo (old talisman collection box) at the shrine.
  • Returning to a nearby shrine: If you cannot revisit the original shrine, check whether your local shrine's collection box accepts ofuda issued by other shrines, following posted signage or published guidelines.
  • Dondo-yaki: A traditional bonfire event held around January 15, where New Year decorations and ofuda are ceremonially burned.
  • Check whether return by mail is available: Many shrines publish postal return procedures on their official websites. Check their published instructions before mailing anything.

This guide does not treat household disposal as a universal Shinto practice. When immediate return is difficult, keep the ofuda clean and respectful until you can visit a shrine collection area.

Summary: Start with One, Treat It with Care

Enshrining ofuda can seem complex, but the first step is remarkably simple:

  • Choose a clean, high spot — south or east facing if possible.
  • A kamidana is nice but not necessary.
  • Jingū Taima goes in the center (or front).
  • Return old ofuda to a shrine after one year.

Consistency beats perfection. What matters most isn't getting every detail right — it's honoring the connection between you and the sacred, in whatever form your life allows.
